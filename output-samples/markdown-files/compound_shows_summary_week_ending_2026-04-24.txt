At The Money: Looking Beyond Market Cap Weighted Indexes
Published: 2026-04-22

In the latest episode of "At The Money," host Barry Ritholtz engages with Rob Arnott, founder of Research Affiliates, to dissect the pitfalls of market cap weighted indexes like the S&P 500, particularly in light of increasing market concentration with stocks like the Magnificent Seven. Arnott critiques the inherent biases of cap weighting, where stocks are bought at high valuations after significant outperformance (up 75% relative to the market) and sold at substantial losses (down 7,000 basis points) when they fall below thresholds. He highlights hidden costs, such as a 15 basis point annual drag from trading due to index turnover (3-5% annually), exacerbated by front-running during S&P additions and deletions, a phenomenon he documented in his 1986 article "S&P Additions and Deletions: A Market Anomaly."

Arnott introduces alternative indexing strategies, notably his fundamental index approach (Rafi), which weights companies based on economic footprint—sales, profits, net worth, and shareholder distributions—rather than market cap. This method, adopted by firms like Schwab, Invesco, and PIMCO with over $100 billion in assets, downweights frothy growth stocks like Nvidia (from 7-8% market weight to 1.5% economic weight) and upweights undervalued stocks, creating a value tilt. Compared to cap-weighted value indexes, Rafi has outperformed by 2-2.5% annually over 20 years, beating them in three out of four years with a tight tracking error of 2.5%, even amidst value underperformance periods like the 3,800 basis point lag of Russell Value against Russell 1000 from peak to trough by 2025.

For investors wary of market concentration and valuation risks, Arnott advocates for fundamental indexing as a solution with a better risk and valuation profile than traditional cap-weighted or even equal-weighted indexes, which carry small-cap and value biases but lack the economic grounding of Rafi. He notes the costly flip-flop phenomenon in S&P indexes, where 28% of additions are dropped within a decade and nearly half of deletions rejoin after dramatic underperformance and recovery cycles. The key takeaway is that fundamental indexing offers a rebalancing alpha—trimming overvalued stocks and buying undervalued ones—potentially making investors over 50% richer than with cap-weighted value indexes over two decades, based on live performance data since 2005.
